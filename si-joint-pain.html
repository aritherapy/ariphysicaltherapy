What the SI joint is and why it hurts

You have two SI joints, where the sacrum, the flat bone at the base of your spine, meets the pelvis. They are strong, tightly braced joints that move only a little, and they pass the weight of your upper body down to your legs. They can get irritated by:

  • Pregnancy, when hormones loosen the pelvic ligaments and your posture changes. Pain at the back of the pelvis is very common in pregnancy. See back and pelvic pain during pregnancy.
  • A fall onto the buttock, or a jolt like missing a step.
  • Repeated one-sided loading from running, lifting or twisting.
  • Changes in how you move after a hip, knee or foot problem, or after a low back fusion.
  • Arthritis, including types of inflammatory arthritis that affect the SI joints.

Is it really “out of place”?

Many people are told their SI joint has “slipped” or is “out.” Because these joints are so tightly braced, they rarely move out of place. The pain usually comes from an irritated joint and the ligaments and muscles around it, and it often settles with the right strengthening and movement.

SI joint pain can also be hard to tell apart from pain coming from the low back or the hip, so your evaluation checks all three. See our sciatica and back pain and hip pain guides.

How physical therapy helps

Treatment usually combines hands-on care to ease pain, exercises that strengthen the hip, buttock and deep trunk muscles so the pelvis is better supported, and easier ways to do the things that flare it up, like stairs, getting dressed and turning in bed. A support belt helps some people for a while, especially during pregnancy, and your therapist can tell you whether one is likely to help you. After your baby arrives, see postpartum therapy.

When to see a doctor first

See a doctor promptly if you have:

  • Numbness in the groin, buttocks or inner thighs, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels. These can be signs of cauda equina syndrome: get emergency care
  • Leg or foot weakness that is getting worse
  • Back or buttock pain with fever, chills or feeling unwell, which can be an infection
  • Back or buttock pain that started at a young age, has lasted for months, comes with morning stiffness that eases with movement, and wakes you in the second half of the night, which can be inflammatory arthritis
  • Pain after a fall or accident, especially if you have osteoporosis, or with a history of cancer or weight loss you can’t explain
  • During pregnancy: vaginal bleeding, leaking fluid, or back pain that comes and goes in waves, which needs a call to your OB right away
