What a herniated or bulging disc is

Discs are the cushions between the bones of your spine. Each one has a tough outer ring and a softer center.

  • A bulging disc spreads out a little past its normal edge, a bit like a tire that’s low on air. Bulges are very common and often cause no symptoms at all.
  • A herniated disc means some of the soft center has pushed through a weak spot in the outer ring. You may also hear it called a protrusion, an extrusion, a ruptured disc or a “slipped disc,” though discs don’t actually slip out of place.
  • A “pinched nerve” happens when disc material presses on or irritates a nearby nerve root. That causes pain, tingling or numbness that travels down the leg, called sciatica.

If your main problem is pain running down your leg, see our sciatica and back pain guide. This page focuses on the disc itself.

What your MRI does and doesn’t tell you

MRIs are very sensitive. Disc bulges and signs of disc wear show up in many people who have no back pain at all, and they become more common with age, a bit like gray hair. So a bulge on your report may or may not be the reason you hurt.

Your symptoms, a hands-on exam and how you move tell us much more about what to do next. If you have a report, bring it to your first visit and we’ll explain it in plain language. Read more: Do I need an MRI for back pain?

Why exercise helps

Rest can feel like the safe choice, but long rest usually leaves your back stiffer and weaker. Many people find that certain movements, such as gentle backward bending, ease their pain, while others do better bending slightly forward. We help you find yours, then add strength for your hips, legs and trunk so your back can handle sitting, lifting and work again. See Is it safe to exercise with a herniated disc?

What treatment at ARI looks like

1
A one-on-one evaluation

Your first visit takes about 45 to 60 minutes. We go over your history and any scans, test how your back moves, and check the nerves to your legs for strength, reflexes and sensation, so we know how the disc is affecting you and can rule out anything that needs a doctor.

2
Calm it down, then build it up

Movements and positions that ease your pain and draw symptoms up out of your leg, hands-on treatment, and changes to how you sit, sleep and get out of bed. As the pain settles, we add progressive strengthening for your back, hips and legs.

3
Back to lifting and living

Practice with the bending, lifting and carrying your work and home life need, a plan for flare-ups, and a home program so you know what to do if your back acts up again.

When to see a doctor first

See a doctor promptly if you have:

  • Numbness in the groin, buttocks or inner thighs, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels. These can be signs of cauda equina syndrome: get emergency care
  • Weakness in a leg or foot that is getting worse, such as your foot slapping or catching when you walk
  • Pain, numbness or weakness in both legs
  • Back pain with fever or chills, or feeling generally unwell
  • Back pain after a fall, car accident or other injury, especially if you have osteoporosis or take steroid medicine long term
  • A history of cancer, weight loss you can’t explain, or constant pain that is worse at night and doesn’t ease with rest or changing position
