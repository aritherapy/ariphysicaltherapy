Toe walking is common in toddlers, and many children grow out of it. When a child keeps walking on their toes past the toddler years, their calf muscles can get tight, which makes flat-footed walking harder. Some toe walking has no clear cause. Other times it is a sign of something that needs a doctor's evaluation, so talk with your pediatrician first. A physical therapist can help with calf flexibility, strength, balance and walking pattern. When toe walking is common, and when to get it checked

Many toddlers walk on their toes now and then while they learn to walk. Most move to a heel-to-toe pattern as they get steadier. If your child still walks on their toes most of the time after about age 2, it is worth talking with your pediatrician.

When doctors cannot find a cause, it is often called idiopathic toe walking, which means toe walking with no known reason. These children can usually stand with their heels down when asked, and toe walking sometimes runs in families. But toe walking can also be linked to other conditions, including problems with the nerves, muscles or development. That is why a doctor's evaluation comes first. ARI does not treat neurological conditions. If your pediatrician finds another cause, they will guide you to the right care.

In older, active kids, heel pain can also lead to walking on the toes. See our page on youth sports injuries.

How physical therapy helps

For children whose doctor has checked them, physical therapy focuses on the parts of walking that toe walking affects:

  • Calf flexibility: gentle stretches for the calf and ankle, built into play and daily routines.
  • Strength: exercises for the muscles at the front of the shin, the hips and the core, which help with heel-first walking.
  • Balance: games that help your child feel steady with their whole foot on the ground.
  • Walking practice: fun drills like walking on heels, marching, walking up ramps and stepping over obstacles to practice a heel-to-toe pattern.

Your therapist will also watch how your child runs, jumps and plays, since toe walking can go along with other movement challenges. See our pages on gross motor delays and coordination problems in kids. Some children need other treatments, which their doctor can discuss with you.

What treatment at ARI looks like

1
A one-on-one evaluation

The first visit takes about 45 to 60 minutes. We ask about your child's birth, development and when the toe walking started, then check calf flexibility, strength, balance and how they walk, run and play.

2
Stretch, strengthen and practice

Play-based stretching, strengthening, balance and walking practice at each visit, with you learning alongside, plus a home program of short, fun activities.

3
Lasting habits

We track flexibility and walking over time, adjust the plan as your child improves, and keep your pediatrician updated on progress.

When to see a doctor first

Talk with your child's doctor before starting therapy, or promptly, if your child:

  • Toe walks on only one side
  • Started toe walking after already walking flat-footed
  • Cannot put their heels flat on the floor, even when standing still
  • Has stiff or very tight leg muscles, weakness, or trouble getting up from the floor
  • Loses skills they used to have, or has delays in other areas of development, such as speech or play
  • Has pain, a limp, or seems to be getting worse instead of better
